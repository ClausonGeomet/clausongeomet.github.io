GEOMETALLURGICAL TESTWORK OPTIMISER / FICTIONAL RESEARCH BETA

Which available core should we test next?

A good testwork programme has to answer more than “which sample looks interesting?” We need geological coverage, suitable physical specimens, a credible budget and a reason to believe another test might improve what we know.

The interactive DH Optimiser illustrates a two-stage approach: first establish feasible geological coverage, then use Bayesian predictive Information Gain to choose further tests as laboratory results become available.

THE PLANNING QUESTION

Coverage first. Learning second.

One sample may fill a missing geological domain. A different sample may have a greater model-expected effect on uncertainty at the prediction targets.

Neither is useful if the physical material cannot actually be tested. This tool makes those distinctions inspectable rather than hiding them behind a single score.

TWO CONNECTED STAGES

From material availability to model learning.

STAGE A / SPECIMEN PLANNING

Can we test it, and what geology is missing?

Assess declared core inventory, laboratory requirements, availability, remaining sample mass, shared stock, geological domains and coverage. The planner proposes an affordable response-blind coverage programme.

Try the fictional D1 → B1 example: two proposed tests cost AUD 80 and AUD 140, exhausting the AUD 220 budget. No material is actually purchased and neither test is assumed to have returned a BBWi result.

A real inventory needs owner and laboratory verification. Unverified stock remains unknown or conditional, not automatically eligible.

STAGE B / SIMULATED LABORATORY

What did the results teach us?

Start with returned fictional BBWi observations, propose a coverage specimen, make a simulated purchase, reveal only that selected measurement and refit the observed-only Gaussian-process (GP) model.

After the coverage requirement is met, the existing information-gain method can propose another eligible and affordable test. The interface shows charged cost, expected uncertainty reduction and post-result model uncertainty separately.

This is a separate fictional scenario from D1/B1; it has its own budget and frozen response oracle.

WHY INFORMATION GAIN?

Expected learning depends on the target.

The GP estimates covariance between possible observations and geological prediction targets. The simplest question is: how much should conditioning on a candidate reduce uncertainty about those targets?

IGw(S) = ½ Σt wt log₂(vt / v′t|S)

This is a weighted sum of marginal target entropy reductions under the fitted model—not joint target-vector mutual information, economic Expected Value of Sample Information (EVSI), or a guarantee that future observed prediction error will be smaller.

Two individually informative tests may overlap in what they teach the model. The selected combination also needs to respect laboratory costs and physical constraints. The fictional examples show those mechanics, without establishing policy superiority or operational calibration.

If you want a shorter explanation with symbol definitions and a worked example, start with the Beat the Sampler challenge and its public How it works guide ↗.

Important limitations. The BIF geology, laboratory outcomes, sample-stock declarations and prices used in the demonstrations are fictional. The GP suitability state is LIMITED; its uncertainty is not certified as calibrated for real mining decisions. Proposal costs and expected Information Gain are not realised economic savings.

Before using real inventory, verify specimen custody, available material, preparation, laboratory method and cost conventions. Do not upload confidential, proprietary, licensed or personal information to the public demonstration.
